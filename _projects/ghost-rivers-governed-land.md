## Project Overview

*Ghost Rivers and Governed Land* examines how historic water-shaped landscapes continue to influence modern flood risk, infrastructure, settlement, and land management in Stanislaus County.

The project begins with a simple idea: past floodplains, wetlands, riparian corridors, and river systems are not only part of environmental history. Their spatial patterns can still be visible in the way risk is mapped, infrastructure is built, and water is governed today.

The project compares four related geographic views:

1. Historic ecological patterns
2. Modern FEMA flood hazard zones
3. Irrigation and flood-control districts
4. A synthesis showing where these systems overlap

## Research Question

**How do historic water-shaped landscapes continue to influence modern flood risk, infrastructure, settlement, and land management in Stanislaus County?**

## Historic Ecological Memory

The first map uses pre-1900 vegetation data to show how Stanislaus County was shaped by floodplains, wetlands, riparian corridors, and river systems before modern water-control systems.

These historic ecological patterns provide a baseline for understanding how water once moved through and organized the landscape.

## Modern Flood Risk

The second map compares that historic landscape with FEMA flood hazard zones.

These modern hazard areas show where parts of the water-shaped landscape are now represented through systems of risk, exposure, and regulation.

Rather than treating flood risk as something disconnected from environmental history, the project asks whether modern hazard patterns still follow older water corridors.

## Water Management Systems

The third map examines irrigation districts and flood-control districts.

These boundaries show how water has been reorganized into systems of delivery, defense, administration, and responsibility.

They add a governance layer to the physical landscape, showing that water is not only an environmental force but also something managed through institutions and jurisdictional boundaries.

## Water Memory Synthesis

The synthesis map brings the historic ecological patterns, FEMA flood hazard zones, irrigation districts, flood-control districts, and city boundaries into one view.

The overlap reveals that many of the same water-shaped corridors appear across multiple systems.

Historic floodplains, wetlands, and riparian corridors are therefore not simply traces of the past. They continue to influence where flood risk is identified, where infrastructure is located, and how land and water are managed today.

In this sense, water has shifted from being primarily an ecological force to also becoming a system of regulation, delivery, defense, and planning.

## How to Read the Map

The synthesis should be read as an **overlap rather than a timeline**.

Historic ecological patterns are shown alongside modern flood-hazard zones and water-management jurisdictions.

Where several layers occupy the same corridors, the map highlights how historic water-shaped landscapes continue to organize modern risk, infrastructure, and governance.
